# Brand System — My Business Care Team (MyBCAT)

This document is standing context for generating MyBCAT visual assets with a reasoning-capable image generation model. It is built from MyBCAT.com public pages, OB_mybcat positioning captures, MyBCAT RAG content, homepage visual inspection, and read-only AWS infrastructure signals. Use it as a source-of-truth brief when creating website hero images, social graphics, pitch deck visuals, patient-access diagrams, product mockups, and stakeholder/report headers. It is intentionally concrete: it gives image models enough constraints to make useful composition, typography, color, and subject-matter decisions without defaulting to generic healthcare stock imagery.

## 1. Company and audience context

MyBCAT, legally My Business Care Team, provides centralized patient access operations for healthcare practices and multi-location groups. The company answers inbound patient calls, schedules appointments directly into practice management systems, handles patient intake, manages patient recall campaigns, supports front-desk workflows, and gives practice owners visibility into missed opportunities and recovered revenue. The audience includes independent healthcare practices and multi-location groups across optometry, dental, veterinary, dermatology, MedSpa, urgent care, ENT, and related healthcare specialties.

The most important commercial wedge is patient acquisition and patient access, not generic outsourcing. MyBCAT should not look like a VA agency, a commodity call center, or a generic AI receptionist. The more accurate frame is: a Remote Hospitality Center, Patient Acquisition Engine, and Patient Acquisition + Practice Intelligence OS. It sells booked visits, recovered opportunities, consistent patient experience, and operational visibility. It does not sell agent hours as the main story.

The core customer pain is front-desk overload at healthcare practices: missed calls, voicemail leakage, inconsistent scheduling, insurance verification complexity, after-hours inquiries, no-show and recall gaps, and no visibility across multiple locations. Useful visual assets should make the viewer feel: “This company makes patient access calm, measurable, and professionally managed.” The first two seconds should communicate trust, healthcare familiarity, operational control, and revenue clarity.

## 2. Brand personality

MyBCAT’s visual personality should be calm, direct, competent, operator-led, and healthcare-specific. It should feel like a serious B2B healthcare operations company built by practice owners who understand the real pressure at the front desk. It should be warm enough for healthcare, but not soft, cute, or wellness-branded. It should be technical enough to show reporting, dashboards, routing, and systems, but not sci-fi or AI-hype driven.

The visual tone should support copy that is direct and punchy. Strong MyBCAT-style lines include: “Your front desk isn’t the problem. The volume is.” “We pick up. Every time.” “You focus on patients. We handle the phones.” Avoid generic corporate filler such as “comprehensive communication solutions,” “our dedicated professionals,” “helping practices thrive,” “seamless,” “holistic,” and “leverage.”

The brand should visually favor proof over promise: real metrics, answer-rate improvements, recovered appointments, case-study blocks, testimonial thumbnails, and before/after operational clarity. Use numbers carefully and only when backed by approved copy. Examples already used publicly include missed-call leakage, answer rates, recovered appointments, revenue recovered, and cost savings. When generating visuals, the safest proof patterns are metric cards, call-flow diagrams, testimonial cards, dashboard-style summaries, and case-study headers.

## 3. Visual identity and logo treatment

The visible MyBCAT logo uses a circular, orbit-like or signal-like mark with blue, cyan, and purple strokes around a dark center form. It suggests routing, coordination, centralization, and communication loops. In generated assets, do not redraw the logo unless a real logo file is provided. If a logo-like motif is needed, use abstract circular signal/orbit shapes, rounded arcs, patient-access loops, or routing rings inspired by the brand, but avoid inventing a fake official logo.

Use circular and loop motifs to represent the idea that patient demand enters the system, gets routed to the right workflow, and returns as booked appointments, recall completion, or measurable revenue. The motif should feel controlled and operational, not chaotic. Good visual metaphors: call signals flowing into a central operations hub, location nodes connected to one care team, appointment slots being recovered, and dashboards turning noise into clear signals.

Avoid medical-cross clichés, stethoscope clichés, robot receptionists, headset stock-photo walls, generic BPO globe imagery, and anything that makes the company feel like cheap labor arbitrage. MyBCAT should look like an operating layer for healthcare groups, not a staffing marketplace.

## 4. Color system

Use a calm B2B healthcare SaaS palette. The observed website colors are approximations, not official tokens. Official hex values should be extracted from the site CSS or brand files before final production.

Primary colors:
- Deep teal / dark cyan for CTAs, highlights, badges, and system accents. Approximate observed range: #006B6F, #00777A, #004F55.
- Very dark navy / charcoal for major headings, dark panels, and footer-like sections. Approximate observed range: #0A1628, #15222B, #18262F.
- Pale blue-gray for page backgrounds and large cards. Approximate observed range: #F3F8FA, #EEF5F7, #E7EEF1.

Accent colors:
- Muted gold / ochre for metric numbers, proof labels, and performance highlights. Approximate observed range: #DDBB5A, #C49A6C, #B17E4F.
- Light aqua / cyan for small checkmarks, icon strokes, trust badges, and tiny interface details. Approximate observed range: #7EE3EA, #BDEFF3.
- Purple-blue-cyan gradients may appear in logo-adjacent accents, but purple should not dominate the page or asset.

Color application rules:
- Use pale blue-gray or off-white as the dominant background.
- Use dark navy text for headlines and key body text.
- Use deep teal for primary buttons, active states, and system lines.
- Use gold sparingly for metrics or “real results” proof moments.
- Use aqua sparingly for checkmarks and small UI indicators.
- Use dark navy panels only for CTA/footer/report-header moments, not as the entire brand mood.

Avoid bright red, hospital emergency colors, neon green, saturated orange, overly glossy purple gradients, black-heavy cyber dashboards, and pure clinical white everywhere. The brand should feel measured and trustworthy, not urgent-room, consumer app, or crypto SaaS.

## 5. Typography

The exact website font family is not confirmed. Use a modern sans-serif with B2B SaaS credibility. The typography should feel bold, clean, rounded but serious, and highly legible. Good directions: Inter-like, Satoshi-like, Manrope-like, or similar modern grotesque sans. Do not use serif, script, handwritten, playful, ultra-futuristic, or monospace-first typography unless the asset is explicitly a technical/internal diagram.

Headline rules:
- Use large, bold sans-serif headlines with tight but readable line spacing.
- Prefer sentence case or title case depending on asset, not shouty all-caps headlines.
- Use uppercase letter-spaced micro-labels for small badges only, such as “REAL RESULTS,” “FOR 3+ LOCATIONS,” “HIPAA COMPLIANT,” or “PATIENT ACCESS OPERATIONS.”
- Keep hero headlines concrete and outcome-based. Good structure: “Centralized Patient Access for Multi-Location Healthcare Groups” or “One Team. Every Location. Every Call.”

Body and caption rules:
- Use muted blue-gray for supporting text.
- Keep paragraphs short.
- Use clear callout hierarchy: label, headline, subhead, proof metric, CTA.
- Avoid dense blocks of copy in images. If text appears in the image, use 1 headline, 1 subhead, and no more than 3 supporting proof points.

## 6. Photography and imagery

Use real healthcare environments, especially optometry, dental, clinic reception, scheduling, intake, and administration contexts. The best photography direction is documentary-but-polished: a real practice workflow captured calmly, not a staged doctor stock photo. People may appear, but they should feel credible: front-desk teams, healthcare coordinators, practice owners, and patients in non-identifiable contexts.

Good photo treatments:
- Soft-focus clinic or optometry office backgrounds.
- Dark teal overlay gradients for testimonial/video cards.
- Rounded media cards with soft shadows.
- Professional testimonial thumbnails with a play button, small label, and proof copy.
- Humans shown as operators and care coordinators, not generic smiling call-center models.

Use illustration cautiously. If using illustration, make it clean, vector-like, card-based, and system-oriented. Good subjects: patient call routing, centralized team handling multiple locations, appointment recovery, dashboard reporting, practice locations connected to one operations layer, recall campaigns returning dormant patients. Use subtle lines, rounded nodes, and calm colors. Avoid cartoon doctors, mascot imagery, clip-art medical icons, and abstract AI brain art.

Avoid surgical imagery, hospital emergency scenes, fear-based patient imagery, overused healthcare props, crossed-arm doctors, headset walls, offshore outsourcing tropes, robot receptionists, and “AI magic sparkle” visuals. MyBCAT’s story is operational reliability and patient access, not novelty tech.

## 7. Layout and composition

The website uses a polished card-based layout with generous whitespace, soft shadows, rounded corners, and clear CTA hierarchy. Generate assets with centered max-width compositions, large rounded panels, and modular sections. The most reusable layout language is: floating navigation or header, two-column hero, testimonial card, metric strip, service cards, industry pills, and dark footer/CTA panel.

Hero layout pattern:
- Pale blue-gray rounded container.
- Left side: small uppercase label, bold headline, short supporting paragraph, two CTAs or CTA-style buttons, and trust badges.
- Right side: testimonial video card, product/dashboard mockup, or clinic workflow image with dark teal overlay.
- Under the hero: three-column metric strip with gold numbers and short labels.

Operational diagram pattern:
- Left: messy inputs such as missed calls, voicemails, web inquiries, recalls, cancellations.
- Center: MyBCAT centralized care team or patient operations hub.
- Right: booked appointments, recovered patients, unified reporting, location-specific visibility.
- Use rounded cards and directional flow lines, not dense enterprise architecture diagrams.

Dashboard/mockup pattern:
- Show clean summary cards, answer rate, call volume, booking rate, recall campaigns, by-location reporting, and follow-up status.
- Keep the dashboard abstract unless real product screenshots are provided.
- Use realistic but non-sensitive data. Never show patient names, phone numbers, DOBs, insurance IDs, or real client PHI.

## 8. Copy voice inside visual assets

Visual copy should be simple, outcome-driven, and operator-aware. It should speak to the healthcare owner or multi-location operator who feels staffing pressure and wants measurable relief. The voice should be direct, specific, and credible.

Use phrases like:
- “Centralized patient access for healthcare groups.”
- “One team handles calls, scheduling, recall, and reporting.”
- “Every call answered. Every location visible.”
- “Turn missed calls into booked appointments.”
- “Patient access operations built by a practice owner.”
- “One team. Every location. Every call.”

Avoid vague promises like:
- “Transform your practice with innovative solutions.”
- “Dedicated professionals at your service.”
- “Seamless patient communication.”
- “Holistic healthcare support.”
- “AI-powered everything.”

Text in images must be legible and minimal. If an image generation model struggles with exact text, generate the image without text and add text later in a design tool. For final assets, typography should be composited manually when accuracy matters.

## 9. Trust, compliance, and sensitivity rules

MyBCAT operates in healthcare. Visual assets must preserve trust. It is acceptable to reference HIPAA compliance, 24/7 coverage, healthcare coordinators, EHR/PMS scheduling, and secure workflows when those claims match approved public copy. It is not acceptable to show patient-level data, identifiable health information, real phone numbers, insurance details, appointment records, or real practice data unless already public and approved.

AWS and infrastructure signals should inform the seriousness of the company, but should not become the customer-facing brand image. For public visuals, abstract the infrastructure into “secure operations,” “reporting,” “routing,” and “centralized systems.” Do not show raw AWS architecture diagrams, database names, cloud resource names, credentials, logs, or internal system screenshots in marketing images. For internal technical decks, use sanitized diagrams with generic service labels and no secrets.

## 10. Things MyBCAT should never do visually

Never make MyBCAT look like a commodity overseas VA agency, a generic call center, a chatbot widget, a hospital emergency service, a staffing marketplace, or an AI gimmick. Never use robot receptionists, neon AI circuits, fake futuristic holograms, red crosses, cartoon doctors, grinning headset stock photos, crowded dashboard walls, cheap labor visuals, or fear-heavy patient imagery. Never imply medical diagnosis, clinical care delivery, or replacement of doctors. The visual story is patient access operations, not clinical medicine.

Do not over-index on “AI.” AI is part of the operating system, but the customer buys reliability, bookings, recall, answer rates, staff relief, and visibility. If AI appears, it should be quiet and operational: routing, summarization, QA, classification, attribution, or workflow automation inside a managed system.

## 12. How to use this document

When generating a MyBCAT image, paste this full document first and say: “Use this as standing brand context. Do not contradict it. Now create the following asset.” Then provide the specific asset brief: asset type, audience, dimensions, headline, subhead, required proof points, CTA, and whether it should be text-free or include text.

For best results, ask the model to reason through brand fit before generating. If the output looks generic, reject it and name the failure: “This looks like a generic call center,” “This overuses AI visuals,” “This is too clinical,” “This is too consumer,” or “This ignores the teal/navy/gold card system.” Keep those rejections and use them to refine the brand system over time.
