# Brand System — Classic Vision Care

## Brand Overview

Classic Vision Care is a full-service optometry brand serving patients across multiple Georgia locations in the Marietta, Atlanta, and Kennesaw area. The practice combines medical eye care, precise prescriptions, diagnostic technology, eyewear retail, and ongoing eye-health support into a one-stop patient experience. Its core promise is not only clinical accuracy, but classic, quality care delivered through compassion, one-on-one conversation, and personalized education.

The brand serves several overlapping audiences. The first is a Gen X female or head-of-household audience, roughly age 33–55, middle to upper class, well educated, and tech savvy. This audience values high-quality and fashionable eyewear, customizable medical eye care, convenience, and a care team that feels professional without feeling cold. The second major audience is baby boomer women and men, roughly age 55–75, middle to upper class, well educated, and relatively tech savvy. They value reliable medical eye care, comfort, convenience, and a hassle-free experience. The Atlanta audience also skews younger, roughly age 25–35, with brand-conscious male and female patients who respond to style, modernity, and a premium-but-approachable retail experience.

The feeling someone should get in the first two seconds of seeing a Classic Vision Care visual asset is: modern, high-end, authentic, clean, professional, approachable, and quietly luxurious. The brand should feel like a trusted medical practice and a fashionable optical destination at the same time. It should never feel like a discount optical shop, a cold hospital system, or a generic healthcare template. The visual system should communicate that CVC is clinically capable, technologically current, stylish enough to support premium eyewear, and human enough to make patients feel seen.

## Visual Identity

Classic Vision Care’s visual identity centers on a clean, modern wordmark paired with an abstract eye-and-leaf icon. The icon is formed from overlapping curved shapes in the brand’s teal, aqua, and green-blue palette. It suggests vision, health, freshness, clarity, and care without becoming literal or overly medical. The primary wordmark reads **CLASSIC VISION CARE** in uppercase, set in a thin, widely spaced, modern sans-serif style. The logo system includes horizontal, stacked/compact, and reversed variations, including light-background versions and dark-background versions with white wordmark treatment.

The logo may be adapted for design purposes, but adaptation should preserve the core recognition cues: the abstract curved eye/leaf mark, the uppercase modern wordmark, the teal/aqua color family, and the clean premium spacing. When the logo appears on light backgrounds, the icon may retain its colored segments and the wordmark should remain dark or black for legibility. When the logo appears on dark teal, black, or deep blue-green backgrounds, the wordmark should reverse to white while the icon remains colored or is adapted with sufficient contrast. Logo changes should be functional design adaptations, not reinventions. Avoid distorting the mark, changing its proportions, adding heavy effects, or replacing the refined sans-serif wordmark with decorative type.

The broader aesthetic is a clean medical-retail system: teal-based, fresh, bright, professional, geometric, rounded, and spacious. It should use strong white space, large blocks of brand color, clean editorial hierarchy, and rounded abstract shapes. Visuals can borrow from healthcare, wellness, premium retail, and modern service-brand design, but should remain grounded in optometry and patient trust.

## Color System

The approved primary palette is a teal and aqua system:

- `#52C7E1` — light aqua/cyan accent. Use for highlights, small accents, callouts, energetic details, or bright portions of the logo and graphic system.
- `#3294A3` — medium teal. Use for secondary panels, icons, buttons, supporting graphics, and balanced color areas.
- `#15737A` — deep teal. Use for stronger brand fields, section dividers, structural blocks, and grounded accent areas.
- `#114755` — dark blue-green. Use as the premium anchor color for large backgrounds, sidebars, hero panels, headers, reversed logo applications, and high-contrast layouts.

The guide also shows a neutral stack of very light gray or near-white, dark gray, black, and white, but does not provide exact neutral hex values. Until final neutral hex values are confirmed, use white or near-white backgrounds generously, use dark gray or black for body copy, and use deep teal for branded contrast instead of default black where possible. The brand should generally live in light mode with teal accents, while dark-mode or dark-panel treatments should use `#114755` and white type for premium contrast.

The palette should feel fresh, clear, clinical, modern, and premium. Teal should be the signature, not a minor accent. Aqua should be used with restraint so the work stays high-end rather than juvenile. The guide does not explicitly list forbidden colors; therefore the safest rule is to avoid introducing off-palette colors unless they serve a specific, approved purpose. Do not let emergency red, generic medical blue, harsh neon, muddy brown, or unrelated rainbow palettes dominate CVC assets unless the brand owner explicitly approves that direction. Warm accent stripes may appear sparingly in examples, but the established teal system should remain primary.

## Typography

The approved typography system uses **TT Norm** as the main font and **Montserrat** as the supporting font. Both are clean sans-serif families that support a modern, professional, approachable brand. The typographic personality should be crisp, geometric, legible, and upscale. The brand guide uses large uppercase section titles, thin horizontal rules, clean specimen layouts, and strong contrast between large headings and small supporting copy.

For AI image generation and design briefs, headlines should be short, confident, and spacious. They can use uppercase for major campaign or section headings when the design calls for premium authority, but patient-facing copy should not become aggressive or shouty. Use clear hierarchy: large headline, smaller subhead, concise supporting copy, and minimal captions. Text should have enough breathing room around it; do not crowd the typography into dense blocks. The brand is compatible with bold headline moments, but the weight and spacing should stay refined.

If TT Norm is not available in a design tool, preserve the intended personality with a similar modern geometric sans-serif and flag the substitution for review. If Montserrat is used, keep it clean and structured, especially for labels, supporting text, UI-like details, and small content blocks. Avoid decorative scripts, distressed type, playful novelty fonts, overly clinical default system type, or dense all-caps paragraphs.

## Photography and Illustration

Classic Vision Care prefers authentic, eye-catching stock imagery that aligns with brand messaging. The imagery should be clean, bright, human-centered, and relevant to optometry, eyewear, eye health, and daily life with glasses. The guide’s examples show patients and eye-care professionals using examination equipment, older adults receiving or interacting with care, smiling people wearing glasses, and lifestyle images that show patients using devices or moving through modern life with confidence.

People can and should appear in CVC visuals when the asset is about patient experience, eyewear confidence, family decision-making, senior care, convenience, or clinical trust. The people should feel real, warm, and relatable rather than overly posed or exaggerated. Age ranges should reflect CVC’s actual audiences: younger brand-conscious adults, Gen X heads of household, and baby boomer patients. Imagery should support the message: fashionable eyewear, reliable medical eye care, comfortable visits, advanced diagnostic technology, or convenient one-stop service.

Illustration and graphic elements should be used as a supporting system rather than a replacement for authentic human imagery. The brand leans toward rounded and abstract designs with a mix of geometric elements. Appropriate illustration/graphic language includes curved wave forms, layered swooshes, circular icon containers, rounded panels, soft geometric fields, and clean line icons. The design should feel professional and clean within the established color palette while remaining approachable and slightly luxurious.

Avoid cold, generic hospital stock photography; exaggerated lens-flare wellness imagery; robotic AI visuals; cluttered clinical equipment shots with no human warmth; cheap coupon-ad imagery; and stock photos that do not connect clearly to the message. Do not use real patient data, identifiable unapproved patients, screenshots containing protected information, or plausible fake medical records in design assets.

## Layout and Composition

Classic Vision Care’s layout style is structured, spacious, and clean. The brand guide itself uses strong vertical sidebars, large teal color fields, white content zones, thin horizontal rules, clear title blocks, and generous breathing room. The system works best when layouts feel editorial and premium rather than crowded. Use white space intentionally. Let one dominant message lead, then support it with one or two concise secondary elements.

Composition should favor clean grids, split-screen structures, large color blocks, rounded/abstract framing shapes, and image-plus-text combinations. Text can sit beside imagery, inside high-contrast color panels, or over image areas only when readability is protected. Curved graphic bands, circular icon containers, rounded cards, and soft geometric panels are appropriate. Hard-edged corporate rectangles can be used as structure, but they should not be the only shape language; the brand’s examples clearly prefer smoothness, movement, and approachability.

For hero graphics and campaign assets, a strong pattern is: premium teal field, concise headline, human or eyewear-related image, curved or rounded framing elements, and one short proof or service line. For educational or service graphics, use a clean hierarchy that separates the topic, patient benefit, and call-to-action. For social and retail eyewear content, balance style with trust: a fashionable image can lead, but the typography and palette should keep the asset aligned with CVC’s medical credibility.

The brand should avoid dense infographics, overly busy collages, tiny text, generic stock-template layouts, harsh angular graphics, and unstructured social graphics with too many competing elements. If an asset needs many details, use a sequence, carousel, or flyer rather than cramming everything into a single image.

## Voice in Visual Assets

The CVC voice in visual assets should be compassionate, approachable, friendly, down-to-earth, professional, knowledgeable, experienced, passionate, and dedicated. The copy should reassure patients that they are receiving careful medical expertise without making the brand feel intimidating. It should also communicate that the optical side is stylish, high-quality, and convenient.

Visual headlines should be clear and patient-centered. Good CVC headline directions include comfort, clarity, confidence, convenience, fashion, reliable care, and personalized solutions. Service copy should emphasize patient education: doctors take time to walk patients through treatment options and explain what those options mean. Convenience copy should communicate one-stop eye care, medical and optical services, online eyewear shopping, and the freedom to browse at home. Technology copy should reference advanced care for dry eye, keratoconus, glaucoma/AMD, myopia, computer vision syndrome, infections, and diagnostic testing, but the language should remain understandable to patients. Eyewear copy should highlight frame selection, premium/fashion brands, and accessible quality options.

The tone should be direct but not harsh, premium but not pretentious, friendly but not casual to the point of losing medical authority. Avoid fear-based medical copy, overly clever puns, generic “see clearly” clichés when they are not adding value, and luxury language that feels exclusionary. The best CVC copy sounds like a knowledgeable optometry team speaking plainly to a patient who wants high-quality care and eyewear they feel good wearing.

## Anti-Patterns

The following anti-patterns are derived from the brand guide’s stated positives and examples; the guide does not provide a full explicit “never use” list, so these should be treated as working rules until the brand owner confirms them.

Do not create assets that feel like discount optical advertising, urgent-care flyers, hospital signage, generic insurance brochures, or stock medical templates. Do not overuse red, emergency symbols, red crosses, sterile hospital environments, or generic blue healthcare gradients that erase the CVC teal identity. Do not use decorative, whimsical, distressed, handwritten, or novelty fonts. Do not distort the logo, rebuild it with unapproved colors, add drop shadows or 3D effects to it, or place it on backgrounds where it loses contrast.

Do not make layouts crowded, chaotic, or text-heavy. Do not use tiny medical disclaimers, dense bullet clusters, or multi-message assets where no single idea leads. Do not use overly staged stock photos, visibly fake smiles, irrelevant wellness imagery, or images that do not connect to eyewear, patient life, or eye care. Do not lean into cartoon eyes, robots, futuristic AI clichés, cheap coupon-burst graphics, or generic luxury visuals that feel more like a spa or fashion brand than an optometry practice.

Do not include real patient information, plausible fake medical records, real phone numbers, unapproved provider names, unlicensed photos, unsupported medical claims, or invented performance metrics in generated assets. If an asset references services, treatments, pricing, insurance, or availability, verify the underlying facts before publishing.
